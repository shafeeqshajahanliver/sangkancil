The story

Meet Ben. He is an ordinary boy with one extraordinary talent for drawing, and the playground he loves is about to be flattened to make way for a condominium. When the neighbourhood asks him to paint a mural that will save it, Ben says no. He is too small to matter. So two mysterious aunties hand him a magic paintbrush, turn him into Sang Kancil the clever little mousedeer, and give him until sunset to outwit a tiger, a river of crocodiles, and a grieving Dragon King who has not let anyone celebrate the Lunar New Year in five years.

Cultural relevance

Sang Kancil is not an invented character. He is one of the oldest and best known figures in Malay folklore, and for many of your children he will already be a familiar face.

Sang Kancil is the mousedeer of Malay oral tradition: the smallest animal in the forest, who survives tigers, crocodiles, elephants and hunters using nothing but his wits. His stories have been told across the Malay world for centuries, in Malaysia, Indonesia, Singapore, Brunei and southern Thailand, long before they were ever written down.

Three tales most Malaysian children know

The crocodile bridge

Trapped on the wrong side of a river, Sang Kancil tells the crocodiles the king has ordered a feast and he must count them first. They line up nose to tail, he hops across their backs counting as he goes, and reaches the far bank.

The tiger and the hornets

Cornered by a tiger, Sang Kancil claims to be guarding the king's drum, and offers to let the tiger strike it. The drum is a hornets' nest. The tiger learns the lesson the hard way.

The founding of Malacca

As told in the Malay Annals, a prince watched a mousedeer stand its ground and kick his hunting dog into the river. Struck by such courage in so small a creature, he founded a city on that spot and named it after the tree he was standing under.

What this show does with him

The two tests at the centre of our story, the tiger and the crocodiles, are the traditional tales, kept intact and played for the same laughs they have always got. What is new is the frame. Sang Kancil is no longer in a forest. He is a boy in a playground that is about to be demolished, and the third test he faces is a Chinese dragon who has cancelled five Lunar New Years.

That bridge matters. The show holds Malay folklore and Chinese festival in the same story, set in a contemporary Malaysian neighbourhood, and asks what a child owes the place they grew up in. It is a Malaysian story told to Malaysian children, without being a history lesson.

Why it works across both streams

Children in the National stream will have met Sang Kancil in Bahasa Malaysia readers and in storytelling at home. For many children in the International stream he will be new. Putting the two groups in one production means the first group arrives already knowing the hero, and the second learns him from their own classmates rather than from a worksheet. For a school running both streams side by side, that shared reference point is worth as much as the show itself.

Music

The original ran with a live band. For schools there are three routes, and what you receive differs with each.

Backing tracks

The most reliable option for a large young cast. Tempo is fixed, nothing falls apart if a musician is unwell, and your staff can rehearse sectionally without an accompanist in the room. The trade-off is that the show cannot breathe with the children on the night.

  • Performance tracks for all ten numbers
  • Rehearsal versions with a vocal guide
  • A cue sheet marking every track top and stop point
  • Lyric sheets for the whole cast

Live piano

Far more flexible in performance, because a good accompanist follows a nervous child rather than running away from them. It depends entirely on having a confident pianist available across the whole rehearsal period.

  • Piano and vocal score for all ten numbers
  • An optional percussion part
  • Chord charts for the accompanist
  • Demo recordings for learning the songs
  • Lyric sheets for the whole cast

Full orchestration

Written for a live student ensemble, and better than either option above by some distance. One requirement: your instrumentation and the ability of your players need to be confirmed before the parts are written, because the score is built for the musicians you actually have.

  • Full score and individual parts, written to your instrumentation
  • Parts pitched to the ability of your actual players
  • Piano and vocal score for rehearsals
  • Backing tracks held as a safety net for the run
  • Lyric sheets for the whole cast
